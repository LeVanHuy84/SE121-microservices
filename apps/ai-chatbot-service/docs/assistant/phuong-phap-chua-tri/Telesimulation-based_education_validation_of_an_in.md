## Abstract
Telesimulation integrates clinical simulation and technology-mediated education, expanding access to
supervised training and skill-based teaching, especially when deliberate practice and structured feedback
are  required.  When  we  direct  this  approach  to  the  training  of  psychologists,  especially  in  Cognitive
Behavioral  Therapy  (CBT),  in  which  Socratic  questioning  (SQ)  depends  on  micro-skills  and  qualified
feedback, telesimulation seems promising. This methodological study aimed to develop and validate the
content of a telesimulation instructional program to develop SQ in therapists in continuing education.
The program included a systematization guide, knowledge assessment, synchronous scenarios with a
standardized patient, and structured debriefing, which was evaluated by judges in two rounds for clarity,
relevance, and applicability. After revisions, the re-evaluated items achieved full agreement (RVC/CVR=1.0).
Limitations include evidence restricted to content validity, so a pilot study and research on effectiveness,
transfer to practice, and implementation in different contexts is recommended.
Keywords: health education; continuing education; simulation training; cognitive behavioral therapy.
## Resumo
A telessimulação integra simulação clínica e educação mediada por tecnologia, ampliando o acesso ao
treino supervisionado e ao ensino por competências, sobretudo quando se requer prática deliberada e
feedback estruturado. Quando direcionamos essa abordagem à formação de psicólogos, especialmente em
Terapia Cognitivo-Comportamental (TCC), na qual o diálogo socrático (DS) depende de micro-habilidades
e  devolutivas  qualificadas,  a  telessimulação  parece  promissora.  Este  estudo  metodológico  teve  como
objetivo elaborar e validar o conteúdo de um programa instrucional de telessimulação para desenvolver
DS em terapeutas em formação continuada. O programa incluiu guia de sistematização, avaliação de
conhecimento, cenários síncronos com paciente padronizado e debriefing estruturado, sendo avaliado por
juízes em duas rodadas quanto à clareza, pertinência e aplicabilidade. Após revisões, os itens reavaliados
alcançaram concordância plena (RVC/CVR=1,0). As limitações incluem evidências restritas à validade de
conteúdo, sendo assim recomenda-se estudo piloto e pesquisas sobre efetividade, transferência para a
prática e implementação em diferentes contextos.
Palavras-chave: educação em saúde; educação continuada; treinamento por simulação; terapia cognitivo-
comportamental.

can be a barrier to the development and maintenance of complex skills, requiring innovative
responses  to  expand  the  reach  of  teaching  excellence.  In  this  context,  digital  innovations
have been consolidated as tools to reduce inequalities and connect professionals territorially

Among these strategies, telesimulation has gained prominence for combining principles of
realistic simulation with telecommunication resources, enabling remote and/or synchronous
learning experiences, with learners and facilitators in different locations (Thomas et al., 2021;
Hayden et al., 2018). By reducing geographical and logistical barriers, this modality expands
access to supervised practices and favors the continuity of training, especially when observable
and returnable activities are required (Thomas et al., 2021).
From a pedagogical point of view, telesimulation is based on Kolb’s (1984) theory of experiential
learning, according to which learning is organized in a cycle that starts with concrete experience,
moves to reflective observation and abstract conceptualization, and is consolidated through
active experimentation. In this design, debriefing plays a central role in sustaining a guided
reflection on decisions, behaviors, and reasoning mobilized during and after the experience,
strengthening learning through performance analysis and favoring transfer to future situations
(Watts et al., 2021). However, for this formative potential to materialize, instructional rigor is
necessary: international guidelines, described by the International Nursing Association for Clinical
Simulation and Learning (INACSL), recommend intentional planning, with alignment between
objectives,  resources,  scenario  fidelity,  and  facilitation  strategies,  to  maximize  educational
results (Watts et al., 2021).
In  the  field  of  Psychology,  this  discussion  is  part  of  a  formative  path  that  begins  at  the
undergraduate  level  but  is  decisively  consolidated  through  specialization  courses,  training
programs, and continued clinical supervision. Studies on Psychology training in Brazil point
to challenges in the articulation between theory, practice, and the development of skills for
professional practice (Rudá; Coutinho; Almeida Filho, 2019). At the same time, undergraduate
training  in  Cognitive  Behavioral  Therapy  (CBT)  is  described  as  diverse  and  sometimes
insufficient  to  sustain  the  applied  mastery  of  therapeutic  skills,  leading  to  the  search  for
formative complementation in postgraduate and continuing education (Neufeld et al., 2018).
This  movement  is  especially  relevant  when  considering  that  evidence-based  interventions
require  more  than  declarative  knowledge:  they  require  observable  performance,  qualified
feedback, and repeated opportunities for deliberate practice. Reviews on therapist training
indicate  limitations  of  expository  formats  and  point  that  when  teaching  integrates  active
training, feedback,  and  support/consultation,  it  has  better  results  (Frank;  Becker-Haimes;
Kendall,  2020).  In  CBT  training,  this  demand  intensifies,  as  core  skills  depend  on  refined,
trainable clinical micro skills (Beck, 2021; Roth; Pilling, 2008). In this context, guided discovery
is  organized  as  a  mode  of  conduction  that  supports  the  client  to  examine  meanings  and
alternatives based on their own experiences, and the Socratic questioning (SQ) is one of its
central  expressions,  not  as  a  “script  of  questions”,  but  as  a  technical  posture  of  curiosity,
collaboration, precision and sensitivity to the context (Kazantzis et al., 2018; Padesky, 2019;
Overholser, 2010). Studies with trainee clinicians indicate that guided discovery is one of the
most difficult skills to master, with obstacles related to hurried driving, excessive directivity,
and  difficulty  balancing  clinical  direction  and  collaboration  (Waltman et al., 2017).  Thus,
telesimulation  emerges  as  a  promising  alternative  to  organize  training  experiments  with
greater  standardization  and  direct  observation;  however,  educational  effectiveness  also
depends on authenticity and careful scenario design, including Standardized Patient training
(SP) (Kühne et al., 2018).
Despite this set of recommendations, few proposals to systematize the use of telesimulation
have  been  implemented,  including  SP  and  structured  debriefing,  for  teaching  specific
therapeutic skills in CBT training, especially in postgraduate and continuing education. Thus,
this study aimed to develop and validate, on content grounds, a telesimulation-based training
program  to  promote  the  development  and  improvement  of  therapeutic  skill  in  Socratic
questioning among therapists in training in CBT.

## METHODS
The study adopts a mixed methodological approach that combines quantitative and qualitative
elements. It is divided into two distinct stages: the first focused on developing the training
program and the knowledge assessment tool, while the second focused on content validation
by expert judges. The analysis aimed to evaluate the quality of the training content and the
evaluation of knowledge considering its comprehensiveness, clarity, pertinence, objectivity
and practicality, according to previously established methodological guidelines (Alexandre;
## Coluci, 2011).
Considering the educational objective of the training and the guidelines for good practices
in simulation suggested by Watts et al. (2021), Debriefing for Meaningful Learning (DML) was
adopted as a debriefing model, given the need for a structured strategy for post-simulation
reflection (Dreifuerst, 2015). The DML favors active learning by organizing reflection in stages
that explain reasoning, decisions, possible alternatives, and application of learning. In addition,
the model uses Socratic-style questioning to lead the participant to the construction of insights
about their own performance, also functioning as a modeling resource for the guided discovery
process and, therefore, as a facilitator of learning Socratic questioning in CBT training.
Instruments and materials
The following tools and materials were used in this study:
- Sociodemographic survey form and background.
It was developed by researchers and covers items such as gender, age, education, degree, year
of completion of the last degree, area, place, and current time of work, teaching experience,
and time of work in this specific area.
- Rating scale
Two content evaluation scales were used in two rounds. In the first round, a scale of agreement
was used with “adequate” and “not adequate” response options, accompanied by a space for
suggestions for improvement and corrections. In the second round, the assessment tool was
configured as a 6-point Likert scale to measure the degree of agreement with the evaluated
items, and the response options ranged from “totally inadequate” to “totally adequate”. In
addition, a space for suggestions and corrections was included for each question evaluated,
allowing the expert judges to provide qualitative feedback on answers deemed inadequate.
Data collection procedure
Two distinct stages were outlined during the data collection procedure:
-    The  first  stage  was  dedicated  to  preparing  and  planning  the  training,  alongside  the
conception  of  the  knowledge  assessment  tool,  through  a  bibliographic  survey.  The
main  reference  work  adopted  was  Socratic  Questioning  for  Therapists  and  Counselors
(Waltman et al., 2017), chosen for its relevance to the compilation of essential aspects for
the development of SQ skill, as well as for its recent publication and for being written by
renowned authors in the field of CBT. The bibliographic survey, which justified the training,
was conducted in the PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Lilacs databases in February
2022, and it resulted in three pertinent articles (Braun et al., 2015; Kazantzis et al., 2018;
Waltman et al., 2017). The choice of these articles was mainly based on the alignment the
research content and the training objectives.
- The second stage consisted of content validation, in which 5 expert judges in psychology
and/or  psychiatry  evaluated  the  contents  of  the  training  and  knowledge  assessment.
This  number  was  considered  adequate  for  the  research’s  purposes,  according  to  the
recommendations  established  (Lynn, 1986).  Invitations  were  sent  by  email,  followed  by
sending the Informed Consent Form (ICF), to the participants. Communication and sharing
of materials with the judges were carried out through the REDcap® platform, owned by the
Israeli Faculty of Health Sciences (FICSAE). The deadline for the two rounds of evaluation
was 15 calendar days after receiving the complete material. However, during the first round
of evaluations, some judges requested an extension of the deadline by 15 calendar days,
which the researchers accepted and granted to all participants.

The inclusion criteria adopted for selecting the population of expert judges were as follows: a)
academic background in psychology and/or psychiatry; b) Doctorate degree in psychology and/
or related areas; c) professional experience in the approach to Cognitive Behavioral Therapy
(CBT); d) acting as a teacher in the area of psychology and/or related areas; e) involvement
in research activities. Regarding teaching experience, professors with at least 5 years of CBT
experience were considered.
The expert judges received guidance on how to proceed and information on the criteria for
evaluating the contents when they had access to the materials for validation. In this study, the
criteria of comprehensiveness, clarity, pertinence, objectivity, and practicability were adopted,
as proposed by Alexandre and Coluci (2011).
The  data  collection  procedure  of  the  second  stage  was  conducted  through  the  REDcap®
platform and consisted of two rounds of evaluations. The deadline established for the first
round of evaluations was 30 calendar days from the submission of the complete material.
After this period, a statistical analysis was conducted, indicating the need for a second round
of evaluations due to disagreement among the judges on some items, as well as suggestions
for changes in parts deemed inadequate. After statistical analysis, the researchers reviewed
and justified each judge’s suggestion, deciding which changes to accept and which to reject.
The first round took place between December 2022 and January 2023. In the second round, the
evaluations focused on the aspects that did not reach the necessary coefficient of agreement
in the previous stage, as well as on the changes suggested and accepted by the researchers.
From that point on, no further changes were made. The second round of evaluations occurred
between March and April 2023.
## RESULTS
Training systematization tool
A  systematization  document  was  developed,  which  pedagogically  organizes  the  training,
describing  its  stages,  objectives,  application  logic  and  guidance  for  conduction,  aiming  to
favor clarity for those who implement and consistency for those who participate and greater
replicability  of  the  program.  The  organization  followed  the  “5-part”  model  proposed  by
Stolovitch and Keeps (2011), used in instructional design to support the acquisition of new
knowledge based on principles that guide engagement and learning: explaining why to learn
(reason  and  expectation),  defining  what  will  be  achieved  (objectives),  building  a  coherent
structure  (sequencing  and  retention),  stimulation  of  the  participant’s  active  response,
provision of task-oriented feedback and reward (intrinsic and/or extrinsic) as reinforcement
of involvement and achievement.
Based  on  this  logic,  the  document  was  organized  into:  (a)  Justification,  articulating  the
relevance  of  therapeutic  skills  in  SQ  for  the  psychotherapeutic  process  in  CBT  and  its
relationship  with  therapeutic  objectives  and  clinical  results  (Heiniger; Clark; Egan, 2018;
Vittorio et al., 2022; Braun et al., 2015; Kazantzis et al., 2018; Waltman et al., 2017);  (b)
Learning objectives, supported and defined by Bloom’s Taxonomy (Bloom, 1956) and based
on contemporary systematizations of SQ and its application in CBT, with an emphasis on
observable skills at the end of the training (Watts et al., 2021) and (c) Training stages, planned
to organize the training path in a progressive and coherent way, favoring comprehension,
guided  practice  and  consolidation  of  the  repertoire  element  (Stolovitch;  Keeps,  2011;
Waltman et al., 2021).
Knowledge acquisition tool
The “Assessment of SQ knowledge in CBT” is a tool to assess the participants’ knowledge. It
contains a clinical vignette that describes a case of anxiety and six questions related to the
knowledge and application of SQ, four of which are objective and two are application.
Telesimulation scenarios
The scenarios were developed considering the content to be trained and the telesimulation
mode with SP. In these scenarios, the participant interacts with a professional actor/actress

in  the  role  of  client,  allowing  to  simulate  the  conduction  of  guided  discovery  from  the
application of SQ (Kühne et al., 2018). As a starting point, the facilitator presents the fiction
contract and netiquette with the aim of aligning expectations, favoring psychological safety,
and organizing participation in the online environment, a practice described and validated
by Watts et al. (2021).
The central focus of telesimulation is the application of SQ in guided discovery. Common CBT
tools, such as mood tracking (emotional recording) and Dysfunctional Thought Record (DTR),
were used to bring the simulated experience closer to clinical practice while guiding Socratic
investigation and the identification of relevant cognitions during the session.
The  clinical  vignette  describes  the  case  of  a  35-year-old  woman,  married,  executive  of  a
multinational company, with anxiety and symptoms associated with panic attacks (tachycardia,
sweating, shortness of breath and catastrophic thoughts). The first scenario was set in the
3rd session of psychotherapy, based on mood tracking performed between the 2nd and 3rd
sessions. Its objectives were to demonstrate the structured conduct of SQ, focusing on key
cognitions  and  phenomenological  understanding,  and  its  non-technical  objectives  were  to
demonstrate the use of monitoring to guide intervention and sustain guided discovery while
avoiding  overly  directive  conductions.  The  SP  profile  was  defined  to  maintain  consistency
with  the  expected  clinical  presentation  (e.g.,  social  dress  and  behavioral  signs  of  anxiety/
hypervigilance).
The second scenario was set in the 4th session, starting from the key cognition identified
earlier.  In  this  stage,  the  participant  uses  the  DTR  to  advance  in  the  stages  of  SQ
(collaborative curiosity, synthesis and closure), evidencing its complete application. Non-
technical objectives included collaboration, empathetic listening, and congruent nonverbal
communication  (soft  facial  expressions).  In  both  scenarios,  interactions  were  scripted  to
guide the performance of the SP and ensure pedagogical consistency, including a “turning
point” (change planned during the simulation) to help the participant to redirect their focus
to  the  central  educational  objective.  Each  scenario  was  planned  to  last  20  minutes  with
participant-customer  interaction.  The  scenarios  were  developed  and  planned  based  on
international  guidelines  for  clinical  simulation,  aiming  to  achieve  learning  outcomes  and
reduce operational failures (Watts et al., 2021).
Debriefing template
The choice of the debriefing model was evidence-based, aligned with the learning objectives
and the simulation mode, contributing to standardizing the facilitator’s training. According to
the guidelines of Watts et al. (2021), the debriefing is the reflective stage of the simulation,
conducted  immediately  after  the  experience  by  a  trained  facilitator.  In  this  program,
the  time  allocated  to  debriefing  was  twice  the  time  allotted  to  each  scenario,  favoring
reflective thinking, performance feedback, and transfer of learning, in line with Kolb’s (1984)
experiential theory.
In  light  of  these  guidelines,  the  Debriefing  for  Meaningful  Learning  (DML)  model  was
adopted (Dreifuerst, 2015). The choice was also justified by its approach to learning based
on Socratic questioning, which accesses the thinking underlying the actions and favors the
modeling of skill in training, promoting reflection in, on, and after the action, in addition to
functioning as a model for the application of the SQ to the participant themself. The DML
is organized into six actions (involve, explore, explain, elaborate, evaluate and expand)
and provides visual support by annotations with color markings: black (what happened/
was said), red (opportunities for improvement/errors), green (positive/correct/effective
points)  and  blue  (reflections,  changes  and  new  perspectives),  in  addition  to  materials
specific to the model. The researchers obtained authorization to use the DML materials
on 08/16/2023.

writing, clarity, applicability, and organization, ensuring its consistency and alignment with
educational objectives.
For  example,  the  analysis  of  the  comments  showed  that  the  judges’  recommendations
focused mostly on clarity, operational details, and standardization, with an emphasis on the
program’s replicability. Among the recurrent suggestions, the following stood out: (i) include/
anticipate  psychoeducation  and  conceptualization  of  Socratic  questioning  (SQ)  and  make
explicit what is meant by “structured” conduction; (ii) make the debriefing (concept, timing
and dynamics of execution) more explicit, including guidelines on how the questions would
be conducted and how the group’s participation would be organized; (iii) standardize times
and  descriptions  between  documents  (especially  duration  of  scenario  and  debriefing)  and
indicate, throughout the stages, where each material (e.g., door briefing sheet) would be used;
(iv) improve elements of the instructional design and the scenario to favor authenticity and
consistency (e.g., detailing the simulated patient’s demand and reason for searching, revising
the wording of frames, and standardizing person/language in tools such as the DTR); and (v)
refine  items  of  the  knowledge  assessment  to  increase  comprehensibility  (e.g.,  formatting,
markups, and alternatives).
In the second round, two judges did not submit their analyses, reducing the panel to five
and raising the critical criterion of agreement to 1.0, as per Lynn (1986), compared to 0.8 in
the  first  round.  At  this  stage,  only  the  items  whose  suggestions  for  improvement  were
accepted were reassessed, with no changes in the knowledge acquisition tool. The reassessed
items reached full agreement (100%) and CVR indices compatible with the validation of the
program’s content.
In the second round of evaluation, all reassessed items showed RVC/CVR = 1.00 across the
dimensions  of  comprehensiveness,  clarity,  pertinence,  objectivity,  and  practicability,
indicating full  agreement  on  the  essentiality  of  the  program’s  content  following  the
implemented revisions. The agreement between the experts was evaluated using Gwet’s AC1
coefficient of agreement, which treated the categories “adequate” and “not adequate” as two
classes. Given complete agreement on some items, the coefficient reached its maximum value,
with limitations for estimating the confidence interval in situations of perfect agreement. Even
so, the results indicate adequate agreement among the experts (Gwet, 2014).
## DISCUSSION
This study developed and gathered evidence on the content validity of a telesimulation-
based  training  program  to  develop  Socratic  questioning  therapeutic  skill  among
therapists in CBT training. Validation by expert judges made it possible to identify areas
for improvement and refinement, mainly in the materials’ clarity, operationalization, and
applicability, resulting in full agreement on the items reevaluated in the second round. In
educational terms, the main finding is not limited to the global approval of the content,
but to the way in which the revisions strengthened instructional coherence (objectives-
activities-criteria) and replicability, aspects necessary when one intends to make formative
interventions  in  specializations  and  continuing  education.  In  line  with  Frank,  Becker-
Haimes and Kendall (2020), training with a higher probability of producing performance
change combines active strategies, guided practice, and feedback, whereas predominantly
expository proposals tend to be insufficient to sustain transfer to clinical practice. Thus,
the adjustments guided by the judges increase the “educational readiness” of the program
by  making  training  opportunities  and  feedback  aligned  with  the  development  of  skills
more explicit and observable.

A relevant point is that the program was structured to make telesimulation a teaching
device  rather  than  just  a  simulation.  According  to  Brandão, Collares and Marin, (2014)
and Kaneko  and  Lopes  (2019),  teaching  by  simulation  requires  balancing  fidelity  and
standardization without losing alignment with educational objectives; thus, fiction contract,
netiquette, SP scripting, and definition of critical points (including “turning point”) work
as mechanisms of pedagogical consistency. Also, according to international guidelines,
learning  outcomes  depend  on  intentional  planning,  alignment  between  objectives  and
scenario design, and conditions for safe and observable learning (Watts et al., 2021). In
addition,  evidence  from  psychotherapy  suggests  that  simulated  patients  can  maintain
authenticity  and  engagement  when  the  SP  is  adequately  prepared  and  the  scenario  is
clinically coherent (Ay-Bryson et al., 2020).
The  choices  related  to  the  debriefing  model  expand  the  program’s  formative  potential
by favoring active learning and transfer to future situations. According to Frank, Becker-
Haimes and Kendall (2020), evidence-based interventions tend to require guided practice,
feedback, and deliberate repetition to produce change in performance. Along the same
lines, Roth and Pilling (2008) highlight the centrality of strategies that make the repertoire
observable  and  trainable.  Thus,  the  debriefing  serves  as  a  space  for  explaining  clinical
choices,  alternatives,  and  criteria,  thereby  sustaining  learning  based  on  observed
performance (Dreifuerst, 2015).
From  an  educational  point  of  view,  the  program  contributes  on  three  fronts:  (1)  it  offers
a  structured  model  for  teaching  a  specific  skill,  reducing  the  dependence  on  exclusively
expository formats, whose ability to modify clinical practice is limited without training and
feedback (Frank; Becker-Haimes; Kendall, 2020); (2) responds to the demand of postgraduate/
continuing education for replicable and potentially scalable strategies; and (3) by incorporating
typical CBT tools (mood tracking and DTR), it increases proximity to clinical routine, favoring
generalization and transference, a central objective in the design of simulation experiences
(Watts et al., 2021; Hayden et al., 2018) element.
Despite its potential, the implementation involves technical and pedagogical challenges.
On  the  technical-operational  level,  unstable  connectivity,  audio/video  failures,  latency,
and unequal familiarity with platforms can compromise the fluidity of the scenario and
reduce  perceived  presence,  requiring  checking  routines,  technical  support,  and  design
with  tolerance  to  interruptions  element  (Hayden et al., 2018; Thomas et al., 2021).  On
the pedagogical level, according to Watts et al. (2021) and Dreifuerst (2015), the quality
of facilitation is decisive for educational outcomes: it requires sustaining psychological
safety, conducting consistent prebriefing, observing performance markers, and conducting
accurate  debriefing  without  turning  reflection  into  an  expository  class.  There  are  also
fidelity/standardization  challenges:  ensuring  SP  consistency  and  emotional/narrative
coherence, especially when training microskills such as question formulation, synthesis,
and response management. As Ay-Bryson et al. (2020) note, clear scripts, SP training, and
controlled  variation  criteria  reduce  noise  and  preserve  comparability.  Finally,  remote
modalities require ethical and institutional attention to privacy, confidentiality, and data
storage, especially when recording.
Evidence should be interpreted as initial, as it refers to content validity. This step is necessary
in  the  development  of  educational  materials,  but  it  does  not  demonstrate,  by  itself,
effectiveness  in  making  changes  in  performance  or  maintenance  over  time.  In  addition,
the number of judges reduced between rounds, which may limit diversity in evaluation and
stability of indicators; the observed full agreement is compatible with the critical criterion
for  smaller  panels,  but  reduces  the  variability  required  for  more  informative  inferential
estimates (Lynn, 1986; Gwet, 2014).
Although consistent with the objective of this stage, the option to prioritize validation by
specialists recognizably favors the program’s internal consistency and theoretical adequacy,
without yet systematically incorporating the perspectives of learners, facilitators, and actors
directly involved in the educational experience, nor addressing its operational feasibility.
In addition, the decision to reevaluate only items with accepted suggestions, in line with
the  review  plan,  may  reduce  the  opportunity  to  test  the  robustness  of  the  set  against

alternative criticisms. Thus, the conclusions are a necessary but insufficient first step to
affirm educational impact, reinforcing the need for piloting and implementation studies.
Future research should move in three directions: (1) pilot study with pre-post assessment
of  knowledge  and,  especially,  observable  skills  (e.g.,  skill  rubrics/checks  applied  to
performance); (2) qualitative data of participants and facilitators (acceptability, engagement,
authenticity,  and  usefulness  of  the  debriefing);  and  (3)  tests  in  different  arrangements
(classes, institutions, and hybrid course modes) to investigate scalability, operational cost,
and  consistency  of  results,  relevant  aspects  for  the  sustained  adoption  of  educational
interventions (Thomas et al., 2021).
## CONCLUSION
By addressing the challenge of translating complex clinical skills into observable objectives,
tasks, and criteria, this study gathered initial evidence of the content validity of a telesimulation
program  for  developing  Socratic  questioning  in  CBT.  Validation  by  expert  judges  not
only  supported  the  theoretical  consistency  of  the  materials  but  also  helped  refine  clarity,
operationalization,  and  instructional  applicability,  resulting  in  a  more  robust  version  for
postgraduate and continuing education contexts.
In addition to technical validation, the findings reinforce a relevant educational implication:
therapeutic  skills  often  treated  as  “intangible”  can  be  operationalized  and  systematically
trained when supported by active methodologies, deliberate practice, and structured feedback.
Telesimulation, when anchored in intentional pedagogical design, can help reduce dependence
on predominantly expository training models and bring training closer to the real demands
of clinical performance.
In  terms  of  training  equity,  the  program  has  the  potential  to  expand  access  to  supervised
training  opportunities  in  scenarios  where  geographical  barriers  and  logistical  constraints
limit face-to-face experiences. By organizing simulated situations with SP, conduction criteria,
and structured debriefing, the proposal can favor more replicable and comparable learning
experiences, supporting the training of therapists in different institutional arrangements and
course modes (online, hybrid, or face-to-face).
Finally, the evidence should be interpreted with caution, as it is restricted to content validity
and  has  limitations  related  to  panel  size  and  the  reduction  of  judges  between  rounds.
These limits point to the need for subsequent studies, including pilot and implementation
investigations,  to  examine  effectiveness,  acceptability,  and  scalability  conditions.  Even
so, telesimulation is a methodologically structured and potentially relevant alternative to
integrate, in a critical and planned way, formative practices aimed at developing therapeutic
skills in CBT.
