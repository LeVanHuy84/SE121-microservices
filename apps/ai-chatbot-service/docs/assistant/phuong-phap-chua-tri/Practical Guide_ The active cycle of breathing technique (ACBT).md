## Introduction
The active cycle of breathing technique (ACBT) is an airway
clearance technique that consists of afive-step cycle with three
essential components: breathing control, thoracic expansion exer-
cises and forced expiration technique (a three-step sub-cycle of
breathing control–huffing–breathing control).
## 1
Breathing control
aims to partially assist the patient to regain the control of breathing
through relaxation and, during the process, gives the patient an op-
portunity to achieve the correct breathing pattern. This is followed by
thoracic expansion exercises, which aim to entrain air into the basal
airways behind the secretions. This is followed by a‘huff’or forced
expiratory breath, which shifts the basal secretions to more proximal
airways. The force of the huff induces dynamic compression of the
airway in the direction of the lung bases, as far as the equal pressure
point (EPP), a point where the pressure inside and outside the airway
is equal. The EPP shifts further towards the more distal and smaller
airways in proportion to the increase in intrapleural pressure and the
duration of the huff. Put simply, secretion mobilisation is effected by a
dynamic shift in the EPP.
Previous and updated reviews confirm equivalence of effects of
the ACBT and other airway clearance techniques (such as positive
expiratory pressure therapy, oscillating expiratory pressure therapy
and manual airway clearance techniques) on lung function, respira-
tory exacerbations and quality of life in people with cysticfibrosis.
## 2–4
A previous systematic review showed that in patients with respira-
tory conditions characterised by chronic sputum production, the
ACBT has a more beneficial short-term effect on sputum wet weight
clearance compared with conventional physiotherapy, external
oscillatory devices and the control groups.
## 5
The benefits of ACBT were
reaffirmed in a recent systematic review.
## 6
This practical guide aims to provide clinicians with practical
recommendations to apply the intervention appropriately, effi-
ciently and effectively. To achieve this aim, this practical guide in-
tegrates  the  results  of  previous  research  pertaining  to  the
intervention together with the authors’clinical experience using the
intervention.  The  recommendations  cover:  patient  selection;
assessment before and after ACBT; education of the patient and/or
caregivers; and the treatment program, which includes the initial
prescription, delivery format(s) (ie, supervision), guidelines for
progression and maintenance strategies.
Application and mechanism
Breathing control
The physiotherapist guides the patient to slow the rate of
respiration while emphasising basal expansion and prolonging
expiration. This breathing control procedure has been shown by
ultrasonography to be associated with a significant reduction in
respiratory rate and improved amplitude of diaphragmatic excur-
sion.
## 7
It is only appropriate to move to the next step (thoracic
expansion exercises) after the patient has at least partially re-
gained control of their breathing, as illustrated by the indicators
detailed inTable 1.

Thoracic expansion exercises
The aim of thoracic expansion exercises is to increase lung volume
but should also target the movement of air to the lower part of the
chest‘below or behind’the secretions. Short and sharp inspiratory
breaths (high inspiratory rate) direct air predominantly to upper
chest alveoli, which are‘fast alveoli’because both airway resistance
and compliance of the alveolar unit are low and consequently a slow
inspiration should be emphasised.
## 8
Three slow inspiratory breaths of
thoracic expansion exercises are typically sufficient to increase the
lung volume enough to shift secretions.
## 1
An end-inspiratory pause
(sometimes referred to as a breath pause) can be considered to
ensure recruitment of‘slow alveoli’in patients with volume loss or
obstructed airways.
## 9
Thoracic expansion exercises may increase the
work of breathing and therefore should be followed by breathing
control, prior to the next energy-consuming stage: huffing.
Huffing–the use of equal pressure point for secretion
mobilisation
Secretion movement in the airway results from a balance between
the driving force of expiration and airway compression (ie, the
alveolar pressure generated by the elastic recoil of the alveoli, intra-
pleural pressure and radial traction on the airway).

reduction in lung volume and compression of the airways.

Low-volume huff
A consequence of airway compression downstream of the EPP is
the induction of increased expiratory airflow generating a‘squeeze’
effect on secretions towards the proximal airways. During a pro-
longed huff, the EPP shifts towards the distal airways as the lung
volume decreases; these mechanics underpin the‘low’lung volume
huff and its effect on distal airway secretions. The longer the huff, the
lower the lung volume reached. If a patient has distal airway secre-
tions, they should be instructed to commence huffing at mid-lung
volume, slightly above functional residual capacity, and perform a
forced expiratory breath to a volume below functional residual ca-
pacity. The distal shift of EPP generates an effective squeeze of se-
cretions from the distal to more central airways, as illustrated in
Figure 2. This should be referred to as a‘low-volume huff’. It should
be noted that a prolonged huff may lead to a low lung volume that
induces airway collapse, particularly in patients with severe chronic
obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) or unstable airways.
Mid-volume huff
A huff that aims to shift secretions from mid-size airways to the
larger proximal airways where coughing becomes effective should
commence at a higher lung volume than the low-volume huff;
however, initiating a huff from maximal lung volume isnotappro-
priate because the high volume above the functional residual capacity
does not increase airway compression, but rather increases the work
of breathing. The main characteristic of the‘mid-volume’huff is its
short and sharp character, whereby the EPP exerts it effect in the mid-
size airways. This should be referred to as a‘mid-volume huff’.
In the authors’experience, should a patient have secretions
located in the distal airways and the two‘types of huffs’are per-
formed correctly, the secretions will be effectively translocated to the
large airways and a cough will be automatically induced.

Huffing is a forced expiratory manoeuvre; to minimise the risk of
airflow obstruction and fatigue following huffing, the huffs should be
followed by breathing control as a recovery phase.
## 1
A huff paired with
breathing control is termed a forced expiratory technique, which is a
three-step sub-cycle of: breathing control–huffing–breathing
control.
Patient selection
The ACBT should be considered for patients with excessive se-
cretions and/or patients having difficulty clearing secretions. It has
been commonly used in patients with cysticfibrosis,
## 2–4
other respi-
ratory diseases (eg, COPD, bronchiectasis or acute asthma)
## 6
and
neuromuscular disorders.
## 12
The ACBT is also recommended for use in
patients before or after surgery, as the thoracic expension exercise
component of the ACBT facilitates lung re-expansion.
## 13
However, the
ACBT is not appropriate for patients with confusion, cognitive
impairment or in patients requiring ventilatory support.
## 13
While the primary effect of the ACBT is airway clearance, the use
of the different components of the ACBT can be independently
emphasised for an individual patient’s needs (see Tailoring section
below). Assessment and re-assessment of the patient is therefore
essential.
## Assessment
Assessment should commence with a patient interview to obtain
direct information that guides the physiotherapy intervention.
Essential question topics are listed inTable 2; these elicit information
to guide intervention, including problem prioritisation, precautions
or modifications and program prescription. Subjective assessment
should be followed by an objective/physical examination that in-
cludes the items listed inBox 1.
Education and manual guidance
Although physiotherapists experienced in the application of the
ACBT may competently instruct patients through the cycle effectively
and efficiently, from the authors’personal experience, students and
inexperienced physiotherapists can struggle tofind appropriate in-
structions to guide the patient to relax and effectively progress
through thefive-step cycle of breathing.
Breathing control
During initiation of the process, the patient should be instructed
to focus on slowing down the rate of respiration; an inspir-
ation:expiration (I:E) ratio of 1:3 is often encouraged. Further,‘getting
the air to the lower part of the chest’,
## 1
rather than just to‘relax’,
should be emphasised. Getting the air to the lower part of the chest
can be facilitated by placement of the physiotherapist’s hand over the
subcostal region, together with the instruction:‘feel where my hand
is and inflate your chest under my hand’. The therapist can facilitate
the expiratory phase by gently pushing the patient’s diaphragm up-
wards and lengthening the expiratory phase by a subtle delay of
diaphragmatic descent; this can be achieved by a gentle supination of
the physiotherapist’s guiding hand. When breathing control takes its
effect (Table 1), the physiotherapist can ask the patient to place their
own hand over the subcostal region to guide the appropriate
breathing pattern.
Students should be specifically taught that breathing control re-
quires more than simply asking the patient to‘relax’and observing
the patient’s breathing for a short period, and then indiscriminately
commencing thoracic expansion exercises. Indicators for successful
implementation of breathing control (Table 1) should be observed.
Thoracic expansion exercise
The goal of thoracic expansion exercises is to enhance lung ca-
pacity, particularly in the lower regions of the lungs. Therefore, it is
appropriate to employ a higher I:E ratio (eg, 2:1) during expansion
exercises compared with that during breathing control. Placement of
the physiotherapist’s hand(s) and application of appropriate tactile
stimulation, particularly guiding lateral costal (rather than anterior)
chest wall expansion; further, increasing the size of the breath over a
sequence of at least two to three breathsbeforereaching near
maximal breath should be emphasised. An end-inspiratory pause
should be considered to optimise alveolar expansion through collat-
eral ventilation, but the patient should be reminded to perform the
inspiratory pause with an open glottis to avoid an excessive increase
in intrathoracic pressure and maintain appropriate airflow for
secretion mobilisation. The use of accessory muscles should be
discouraged during thoracic expansion exercises.
## Huffing
The physiological relationship between the EPP and lung volume
is used to support a recommendation to use‘low-’and‘mid-volume’
huffs to describe the huffing process. The authors recommend that
huffs that shift secretions from peripheral to intermediate airways
should be logically renamed‘low-volume’huffs while huffs that exert
their effect in the mid-size airways should be renamed‘mid-volume’
huffs. A‘high-lung volume huff’is misleading and a redundant term.
The term‘high’volume may be inappropriately interpreted as taking
a‘big’or even maximal inspiration before the huff. This usually re-
sults in an unnecessary increase in the work of breathing and an
ineffective technique.

## Tailoring
While the ACBT is employed as an airway clearance technique,
patients with excessive secretions as their main problem should be
advised to focus on the three-step forced expiratory technique cycle
rather that the full ACBT. Patients with dyspnoea as their principal
complaint should focus on breathing control. Patients with reduced
lung volume (eg, after major abdominal or thoracic surgery) should
focus on the thoracic expansion exercises component. These analyt-
ical concepts should be given more emphasis in physiotherapy entry-
level education and included during clinical placements.
## Parameters
The duration of a single session of ACBT will vary depending on
each patient’s needs. The essential guide is that the patient should
not feel tired from performing the ACBT (ie, the ACBT should not
increase the work of breathing for the patient). Theoretically, the skill
of each step should be appropriately‘mastered’(Table 1) before
moving on to the next step. It is important to note that the different
components of the ACBT are emphasised for different patients. Where
dyspnoea is the main problem, the patient may spend 2 to 3 minutes
engaged in breathing control, needing less time performing thoracic
expansion exercise and may only require one huff to assess the
presence of secretions, despite the patient presenting with a chronic
underlying suppurative condition. In general, one cycle can be
completed in 2 to 3 minutes. The whole cycle or part of the cycle
(emphasised components) can be repeated two to three times, and
thus the duration of each session of intervention may be around 10
minutes.
Session frequency also depends on each individual patient. For
patients with dyspnoea, breathing control can be performed any time
dyspnoea is experienced. Post-surgical patients with reduced lung
volume may benefit from sessions that focus on thoracic expansion
exercises every 1 to 2 hours while awake. Whereas, for patients with
cysticfibrosis or any chronic condition with sputum retention, the
recommended frequency of the full cycle is often two to three times
per day or when the patient feels the need to move and clear their
secretions.
## Format
Introduction of the ACBT must be supervised initially. The authors’
experience is that the skills required for each step of the ACBT must
be explained and mastered before moving onto the next step. When
the patient has followed thefive steps, they should then be asked to
repeat thefive steps (ie, a cycle), and then repeat a cycle again after a
period of rest. In usual circumstances this takes 15 to 20 minutes.
When the patient can recall the cycle and achieve the indicators listed
inTable 1, the physiotherapist should feel confident that the patient
can progress to unsupervised application of the technique. For some
patients, especially the elderly, the full cycle can be introduced over
several separate sessions, but mastering each of the required com-
ponents should be emphasised.
## Progression
There is no‘progression’of the technique as such. Progress does
not rely on mastery of more or longer cycles. The goal is that each
individual patient understands the required cycle mechanism and can
master the skills required to perform each component, and ultimately
can employ the corresponding component in response to the possibly
varying symptom presentation of their condition. One typical
example is that the thoracic expansion exercises component may be
emphasised in the early postoperative stage when reduced lung
volume is a consequence of wound pain, but should sputum retention
become an issue of concern, the huffing component should be
intensified.

## Reassessment
To facilitate optimal performance of the ACBT, outcome indicators
that guide progressive movement through the cycle should be
emphasised (Table 1).
## Maintenance
When the full cycle of the ACBT is mastered, the patient is advised
to continue using the technique whenever symptoms of dyspnoea or
sputum accumulation occur. For patients with chronic respiratory
conditions, the ACBT is an essential lifelong skill necessary for suc-
cessful self-management of their condition.
